\title{Separating $\Bone$ and $\Tone$ in MPME imaging:\\ an intuitive account\\[4pt]
\large Companion to the MPME technical note}
\author{Sarang Joshi}
\date{\today}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
The multi-pathway multi-echo (MPME) sequence of Cheng et al.~\cite{cheng2019} estimates the
transmit scale $\Bone$ and $\Tone$ from two steady-state scans. This note explains, without proofs,
why separating the two is hard and what makes it possible. One scan sees its flip angle only
relative to the tissue's Ernst angle, so a larger flip angle in a tissue with a shorter $\Tone$
looks exactly like a smaller one in a tissue with a longer $\Tone$, at any flip angle and whatever
is assumed about $\Bone$ across the image. Two scans separate them only to the extent that their
samples move at different rates when $\Bone$ changes, a difference we call the lever. The lever
predicts the $\Bone$ bound to within a factor of about five over four orders of magnitude, explains why
small flip angles fail and the $330^\circ$ pulse works, and also governs the aliases. The proofs and the full analysis are in the
technical note~\cite{technote}; the choice of flip angles is in the design note~\cite{flipnote}.
\end{abstract}

\section*{The answer in brief}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{One scan cannot separate $\Mzero$, $\Bone$ and $\Tone$, at any flip angle} (\cref{sec:one}). It
  measures only the offset of its flip angle from the Ernst angle and an amplitude. More echoes,
  a smooth or shared $\Bone$, or more physics in the model do not change this in practice.
\item \textbf{Two scans separate them through the lever} $L=c(x_2)-c(x_1)$, $c(x)=x/\sin x$, the
  difference in how fast the two samples move with $\Bone$ (\cref{sec:two}). Two small flip
  angles have $L\approx0$; a $330^\circ$ pulse has $L=-12.5$.
\item \textbf{With the published protocol, $\Bone$ is no longer the dominant limit on $\Tone$} (\cref{sec:t1}):
  knowing it exactly would lower the $\Tone$ bound only by a factor of $1.45$. A $\Bone$ supplied from
  elsewhere is worth using with both scans only if it is more accurate than the $\Bone$ the two scans
  measure themselves: their high-flip data amplify its error tenfold at $\Bone=1$ and up to about
  $260$-fold near the $360^\circ$ crossing.
\item \textbf{Aliases are the other roots of one equation in $\Bone$} (\cref{sec:alias}). They lie beyond
  a value of $\Bone$ at which a pulse crosses a multiple of $180^\circ$; the $360^\circ$ crossing is where the best
  local $\Bone$ measurement meets the worst global behaviour.
\item \textbf{Two systematic limits are often undetectable in the data} (\cref{sec:invisible}): a
  pulse-ratio error, which every design passes to $\Tone$ with a factor of at least $1.6$, and model
  errors near $360^\circ$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Setting}\label{sec:setting}

Each MPME scan records three configuration pathways, three echoes each, of an unbalanced
steady-state gradient echo; the two scans share $\TR=25$\,ms and the readout and differ in the
nominal flip angle ($15^\circ$ and $330^\circ$); in the published protocol scan 2 covers only the centre of
$k$-space and serves mainly to map $\Bone$. The actual flip angle of scan $i$ is $x_i=\Bone\alpha_i$. The unknowns
are $\Mzero$, $\Bone$, $\Tone$, $\Ttwo$, $\Ttwos$, the off-resonance $\dw$ and a phase $\phi_0$. Precision is measured by
Cram\'er--Rao bounds on the logarithms of the positive parameters (relative standard deviations),
per unit $\sigma/\Mzero$ for complex Gaussian noise; at SNR$(\Mzero)=300$ divide by 300. Numbers refer to the
tissue used by Cheng et al.\ to optimise their protocol ($\Tone/\Ttwo/\Ttwos=1500/70/60$\,ms) at $\Bone=1$ unless
stated. Everything assumes the ideal model of~\cite[Sec.~2]{technote} (instantaneous pulses, one
flip angle per voxel); \cref{sec:invisible} says what happens outside it.

\section{One scan sees only the offset from the Ernst angle}\label{sec:one}

The Ernst angle $\alpha_E=\arccos\E^{-\TR/\Tone}$ maximises the signal of a spoiled gradient echo; for
$\Tone=1500$\,ms it is $10.4^\circ$, for 850\,ms $13.8^\circ$. Put flip angles on the log half-angle axis
$s=\ln|\tan(x/2)|$. On this axis the steady state of one scan has a remarkably simple form
\cite[Thm.~1, Prop.~2, eq.~13]{technote}: every pathway amplitude is the tissue's \emph{amplitude}
$\Mzero\tan(\alpha_E/2)$ times a function of one number (for given $\Ttwo$ and off-resonance), the \emph{offset} of
the sample from the Ernst angle,
\[
  w=\ln|\tan(x/2)|-\ln\tan(\alpha_E/2).
\]
The spoiled-signal curve is a bell-shaped bump ($\operatorname{sech}w$) of fixed shape centred on the Ernst
angle (\cref{fig:bump}); the ratios between pathways tell where on the bump the scan samples, and
the overall scale tells the bump's height. That is all a scan reveals about $(\Mzero,\Bone,\Tone)$; the
echo decays and phases are spent on $\Ttwo$, $\Ttwos$, $\dw$ and $\phi_0$.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{ernst_logtan.png}
\caption{The signal amplitude on the log half-angle axis for three combinations of $\Bone$, $\Tone$ and
$\Mzero$ that give identical scan-1 data (white matter, $\Tone=850$\,ms; scan 1 at $15^\circ$). Raising $\Bone$ moves the sample (circles) to the right;
shortening $\Tone$ moves the bump (dotted: Ernst angles) by the same amount; lowering $\Mzero$ keeps the
height. Squares: the second scan, at $330^\circ$ (a) or $30^\circ$ (b). From~\cite[Fig.~2]{technote}.}
\label{fig:bump}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Why one scan cannot separate $\Bone$ from $\Tone$.} Raise $\Bone$: the sample moves to the right.
Shorten $\Tone$ by the right amount: the Ernst angle grows and the bump moves to the right by the same
distance. Lower $\Mzero$: the height is restored. The whole picture translates rigidly and the data
do not change, at any flip angle, exactly. For small angles and $\TR\ll\Tone$, $\Bone\to\kappa\Bone$ is compensated by
$\Tone\to\Tone/\kappa^2$ and $\Mzero\to\Mzero/\kappa$, the familiar $\Bone$ sensitivity of variable-flip-angle $\Tone$
mapping~\cite{deoni2003} (at $15^\circ$ the exact logarithmic slopes are $-2.02$ and $-1.01$~\cite[eq.~17]{technote}). A $10\%$ error in $\Bone$ becomes an error of about
$-18\%$ in $\Tone$.

\paragraph{Nothing inside one scan rescues it.} More echoes or pathways sample the same point of the
same bump. A smooth (e.g.\ polynomial) $\Bone$ field does not help either: every voxel has its own $\Tone$
and $\Mzero$ to absorb the change, so $N$ voxels give $2N$ equations for $2N+p$ unknowns, and any field fits
the data exactly~\cite[Prop.~7, Cor.~3]{technote}. Voxels that share one $\Bone$ slide together, keeping
their $\Mzero$ ratios exactly and their $\Tone$ ratios to about $10^{-4}$, but their common scale stays free
\cite[Prop.~8]{technote}. Modelling more physics does not help in practice: relaxation during the
pulse leaves the degeneracy exact, and a known slice profile, off-resonance during the pulse or
diffusion give bounds on $\ln\Bone$ 400 to $5\times10^6$ times larger than a second scan does, and no information
at all as soon as the extra physics has an unknown parameter of its own~\cite[Sec.~5.4]{technote}.

\section{Two scans: the lever}\label{sec:two}

A second scan samples the same bump at another point. When $\Bone$ changes, each sample moves along
the $s$-axis at its own \emph{rate}
\[
  c(x)=\frac{\mathrm d\ln|\tan(x/2)|}{\mathrm d\ln\Bone}=\frac{x}{\sin x}\qquad(x\text{ in radians}),
\]
written $c_i=c(x_i)$ for scan $i$, while the bump (the tissue) moves the same for both. The compensation that worked for one scan now
fails unless the two samples move at the same rate. $\Bone$ is therefore visible only through the
difference of the rates, the \emph{lever} $L=c(x_2)-c(x_1)$: the bound on $\ln\Bone$ is the uncertainty of the
difference of the two offsets divided by $|L|$~\cite[Prop.~3]{flipnote}.

\begin{figure}[!tb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{limits_lever.png}
\caption{(a) The rate $c(x)=x/\sin x$ (dotted: multiples of $180^\circ$). Small angles move at rate $\approx1$; a $330^\circ$ pulse is a $30^\circ$ pulse in
disguise ($|\tan165^\circ|=\tan15^\circ$) that moves eleven times faster and in the opposite direction. (b) Bound on
$\ln\Bone$ against $1/|L|$ for scan 1 at $15^\circ$ with scan 2 from $30^\circ$ to $345^\circ$, and for four pairs of small angles
(all seven parameters free). The bound spans four orders of magnitude while $|L|$ times the bound
stays between 26 and 144 (shaded); pulses with the same $|\tan(x/2)|$ (e.g.\ $30^\circ$ and $330^\circ$) give
identical products.}
\label{fig:lever}
\end{figure}

\Cref{fig:lever}b shows that this one number organises the problem: over 19 protocols the $\Bone$ bound
ranges from $1.1$ to $13\,000$, while $|L|$ times the bound stays within a factor of about five.

\paragraph{Why small angles fail.} For small angles $c(x)\approx1+x^2/6$, so two small flip angles have
nearly the same rate and $L\approx(x_2^2-x_1^2)/6\to0$. For $15^\circ/30^\circ$, $L=0.036$ and the $\Bone$ bound is $1023$: the
parameters are identifiable in principle, but separating them needs an SNR of order $10^4$. This is
the leading-order scaling invariance $(\Mzero,\Bone,\Tone)\mapsto(\Mzero/\kappa,\kappa\Bone,\Tone/\kappa^2)$ of small-angle
protocols~\cite[Prop.~4]{technote}, seen as the limit $L\to0$; adding more small angles does not help.

\paragraph{Why the $330^\circ$ pulse works.} A $330^\circ$ pulse carries exactly the information of a $30^\circ$ pulse
about its own offset, but its rate is $-11.5$, so $L=-12.5$. The $\Bone$ bound falls from 1023 to $2.9$, by
the factor $|L_{330}/L_{30}|=351$ exactly~\cite[Prop.~5, Sec.~4.4]{technote}. Any second pulse whose rate differs
strongly from the first scan's, and whose sample still sits on the informative part of the bump,
would do (where the samples sit accounts for the remaining factor of five in \cref{fig:lever}b); it
does not need to be near $360^\circ$ (a $270^\circ$ pulse has rate $-4.7$).

\paragraph{An equation in $\Bone$ alone.} Each scan fixes its offset, i.e.\ the ratio
$\xi_i=\tan(x_i/2)/\tan(\alpha_E/2)$ (up to sign). Dividing the two ratios eliminates $\Tone$ and $\Mzero$:
\begin{equation}
  f(\Bone)=\frac{\tan(\Bone\alpha_2/2)}{\tan(\Bone\alpha_1/2)}=\frac{\xi_2}{\xi_1},\ \text{a number the data fix},
  \label{eq:f}
\end{equation}
the half-angle analogue of double-angle $\Bone$ mapping. Its logarithmic slope is the lever itself,
\[
  \frac{\mathrm d\ln|f|}{\mathrm d\ln\Bone}=L,
\]
so the same number controls how precisely and how uniquely $\Bone$ is determined (\cref{sec:alias}).

\section{What limits $\Tone$ once $\Bone$ is resolved}\label{sec:t1}

\Cref{tab:budget} frees the parameters one group at a time (the factors depend on the order). With
the published protocol, knowing $\Bone$ exactly would lower the $\Tone$ bound only by a factor of $1.45$,
against $75$ for $15^\circ/30^\circ$: most of the uncertainty now comes from the weak $\Tone$ encoding of a $15^\circ$
scan with $\TR\ll\Tone$, shared with $\Mzero$, $\Ttwo$ and $\Ttwos$. The cost of not knowing $\Bone$ has an exact and simple
form,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Var}(\ln\Tone)=\mathrm{Var}(\ln\Tone\mid\Bone\text{ known})+\beta^2\,\mathrm{Var}(\ln\Bone),
  \label{eq:budget}
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ is the factor with which an error in a supplied $\Bone$ reaches $\Tone$ (next paragraph): for the
published protocol $40.5^2=28.0^2+(10.1\times2.9)^2$, and for $15^\circ/30^\circ$, $\beta=-2.06$ but the $\Bone$ bound is $1023$.

\paragraph{A $\Bone$ supplied from elsewhere.} Suppose $\Bone$ comes from a separate map with a small error
and the rest is fitted. With scan 1 alone, the $\Tone$ error is $\beta=-2.0$ times the $\Bone$ error at every $\Bone$,
as in \cref{sec:one}. With both scans the fit must reconcile two samples that move at different
rates, and $\beta=-\bar c/h$, where $\bar c$ is the average of the two rates weighted by the information each
scan carries about its offset and $h=\tau/(2\sinh\tau)\approx\tfrac12$, $\tau=\TR/\Tone$, is how far the bump moves per unit
$\ln\Tone$ (checked against direct computation to $10^{-15}$). For the published protocol $\beta=+2.4$ at
$\Bone=0.8$ and $+10$ at $\Bone=1$; it swings from about $+260$ to $-270$ within a fraction of a percent of the
$360^\circ$ crossing at $\Bone=1.09$, and is $-15$ at $\Bone=1.2$ (\cref{fig:crossing}c). By \eqref{eq:budget}, a supplied
$\Bone$ improves on the joint fit only if its error is below the joint $\Bone$ bound ($2.9\,\sigma/\Mzero$, about $1\%$ at
SNR 300). Otherwise $\Bone$ should be fitted jointly from both scans ($40.5$), or, when scan 2 is
low-resolution as in the published protocol, the map should be used with scan 1 alone ($57.1$,
error transfer $-2$).

\section{Aliases and the $360^\circ$ crossing}\label{sec:alias}

An \emph{alias} is a different set of parameters that produces exactly the same noise-free data; no
SNR separates it from the truth, only prior knowledge or more data. In MPME the aliases are simply
the other roots of \eqref{eq:f}: any $\Bone$ with the same ratio reproduces both scans once $\Tone$ and $\Mzero$
are adjusted, provided the adjusted $\Tone$ exists (its Ernst angle stays below $90^\circ$)~\cite[eq.~18]{technote}. A \emph{complex alias} has the same signed ratio; a \emph{magnitude
alias} has the opposite sign, which negates scan 2 relative to scan 1 and is invisible in magnitude
images. The alias values of $\Bone$ depend on the true $\Bone$ alone, not on the tissue.

For the published protocol at $\Bone=1$ (\cref{fig:crossing}a) the magnitude alias is $\Bone=1.20$, inside
the physiological range: the scan-2 pulse becomes $396^\circ\equiv+36^\circ$ instead of $330^\circ\equiv-30^\circ$. Complex data with a
phase shared by the two scans remove it, and so, for magnitude data, does the sign of the relative
phase of the two FID images (Cheng et al.'s FID-sign test). The complex alias is $\Bone=2.01$, excluded
by the fitting range $\Bone\le540/330$ (scan-2 angle at most $540^\circ$). Because $\mathrm d\ln|f|/\mathrm d\ln\Bone=L$,
aliases arise where $f$ has zeros or poles, i.e.\ where a pulse crosses a multiple of $180^\circ$; a
design whose two pulses stay within $(0^\circ,180^\circ)$ and $(180^\circ,360^\circ)$ over the fitting range has none at all
\cite[Prop.~5]{flipnote}. A $270^\circ$ second pulse fitted within $\Bone\in[0.667,1.333]$ is such a design.

\paragraph{The $360^\circ$ crossing.} The published second pulse reaches $360^\circ$ at $\Bone=1.09$. There the
scan-2 signal vanishes, and scan 2 measures $\Bone$ alone, through the derivative of a null: locally
$\Bone$ is determined best ($0.09$ against $2.9$ at $\Bone=1$, \cref{fig:crossing}b). Everything else goes wrong
there. The $\Tone$ bound rises to the scan-1 value with $\Bone$ known (56 against 41 at $\Bone=1$), because scan 2
now informs $\Bone$ alone and no longer helps with $\Tone$, $\Mzero$, $\Ttwo$ or $\Ttwos$; the supplied-$\Bone$ factor swings
between about $\pm260$ (\cref{fig:crossing}c); the magnitude alias comes within a few percent of the truth;
the likelihood has distant near-equal maxima at the other scan-2 nulls, which account for the large
errors that remain in the phantom after a spatial restart~\cite[Secs.~7 and~9]{technote}; and model
errors bias $\Tone$ most there (\cref{sec:invisible}). A second pulse near $270^\circ$ keeps the crossing outside
$\Bone\in[0.7,1.3]$ and has smoother bounds across the range (dashed), although its own crossing, at
$\Bone=1.33$, is close enough that its effects reappear near $\Bone=1.3$.

\section{Limits the bound cannot see}\label{sec:invisible}

\paragraph{Pulse-ratio errors.} If the two pulses do not have their nominal ratio, as with two pulses
of different shape or duration, the fit absorbs the error exactly: two scans give three numbers for
the three unknowns $(\Mzero,\Bone,\Tone)$, so a shifted offset is reproduced by other values with zero
residual. The $\Tone$ error is at least $1.64$ times the ratio error for every choice of angles ($1.6$--$2.1$ at $\Bone=1$
for the shortlisted designs of~\cite[Table~2]{flipnote}; much more for small-angle pairs)~\cite[Prop.~4]{flipnote}. The ratio must be calibrated; the in-vivo factor $\beta=1.24$ of
\cite{cheng2019}, which multiplies this ratio, cannot be validated from the data.

\paragraph{Model errors near $360^\circ$.} Near $360^\circ$ the effective angle of the second pulse is small, so
small imperfections change it by a large fraction. Unmodelled finite pulses ($1$\,ms for $330^\circ$ at a
fixed peak $\Bone$) bias $\Tone$ by $10$--$20\%$ where the scan-2 angle passes $330$--$350^\circ$, and a $10\%$ flip-angle ramp
across a voxel at the slab edge by $13$--$74\%$ near or across $360^\circ$, often without a misfit that a $\chi^2$
test would flag~\cite[Sec.~6]{flipnote}. Partial volume with CSF gives
biased estimates with falsely confident uncertainty maps~\cite[Sec.~10]{technote}.

\paragraph{Estimators and design.} The bounds are attainable: joint maximum likelihood on complex
data reaches them (SD/bound $0.99$--$1.03$ at SNR 1000--3000), and so does magnitude least squares with the
FID sign, while the published analytic inverse is $3$--$7$ times above them, an algorithmic rather than
an informational loss~\cite[Secs.~6--7]{technote}. For the choice of flip angles, the design note
\cite{flipnote} finds the optimum flat around the published protocol: the angles buy robustness,
not precision, and a second pulse near $255$--$270^\circ$ with $\alpha_1\approx17$--$21^\circ$, finite pulses in the model and a
calibrated pulse ratio is the recommended default.
